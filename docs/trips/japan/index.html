The journey

In September 2025, we spent eight days cycling from the outskirts of Tokyo to Kyoto, taking a deliberately indirect route through the mountains and countryside of central Japan.

Our route was inspired by Raid Cycling's Tokyo to Kyoto itinerary, but we adapted it into a self-guided trip, using trains where they made sense and building alternate routes into some of the hardest days.

Rather than following the busy and more direct corridor between Tokyo and Kyoto, the route heads into the mountains almost immediately. Over eight days, we rode through the mountains of western Tokyo and Yamanashi, followed the historic Kiso Valley, crossed remote rindo roads in the Japanese Alps, visited Takayama and Shirakawa-go, descended toward the Sea of Japan, and eventually turned south for a final mountainous ride into Kyoto.

This is not an easy bike tour. Several days involve thousands of feet of climbing, steep grades and remote roads, and our longest day exceeded 100 miles. But the difficulty is also what makes the route exceptional. Cycling connected places that otherwise might have felt like isolated stops on a Japan itinerary and gave us a chance to experience a quieter, more rural side of the country.

Day 1: Tokyo → Ome → Yamanashi

59.4 miles | 7,359 ft climbing | Max grade: 13%

Difficulty: Moderate

The first challenge of our bike trip was simply getting out of Tokyo.

Rather than cycling from the center of the city, we used public transportation to reach Ome, on the western edge of metropolitan Tokyo. From there, we began riding into the mountains. Before I go any further, there is a topic I must cover. Rinko bags.

Taking Your Bike on Trains in Japan: The Rinko Bag

One of the reasons this route works so well is Japan's extensive rail network—but taking a full-size bike on a Japanese train requires a little more planning than simply rolling it onboard.

On most trains, bicycles must be disassembled or folded and completely enclosed in a bike bag, commonly called a rinko bag (輪行袋). You generally can't wheel an assembled bike onto the platform and board with it. Japan Rail's (JR) current guidance specifies that the bicycle must be completely contained in the bag, with no wheels, saddle, or other bike parts sticking out.

For a road or gravel bike, this usually means removing at least the front wheel—and, depending on your rinko bag, potentially both wheels—then securing the wheels to the frame and putting the entire setup inside the bag. Lightweight rinko bags pack down surprisingly small, which makes it possible to carry one on the bike throughout a multi-day tour and pull it out whenever you need to hop on a train.

The process isn't particularly difficult, but give yourself extra time at the station. Removing wheels, protecting the drivetrain and disc rotors, securing everything together, getting the bike into the bag, and then carrying your bike plus panniers or other luggage through a busy station is considerably more cumbersome than normal train travel. Stairs, narrow ticket gates, crowded platforms, and transfers can make the process especially awkward. JR also expects passengers to carry and manage their own bicycles and luggage.

Once onboard, be considerate about where you put the bike. A packed bicycle is still a large piece of luggage. Look for an appropriate luggage area or somewhere you can keep it securely out of the aisle, and avoid peak commuting periods when possible.

My advice: Practice packing your bike into the rinko bag at home before you leave for Japan. Know exactly which wheels need to come off, how you'll secure them to the frame, and where you'll put your helmet, bags, and other gear while carrying the bike. Bring a few reusable straps or bands, and be especially careful with disc brake rotors and derailleurs.

Most importantly, don't view the rinko bag as an inconvenience—it can actually be one of the best tools you carry. Having one meant we could intentionally incorporate trains into our Tokyo-to-Kyoto route, skip less appealing sections, shorten a day if necessary, and build backup plans around weather or tired legs. Once you get the hang of packing the bike, Japan's rail network gives you an enormous amount of flexibility when designing a self-supported cycling trip.

One caveat: Rules can vary by railway operator and train type, so check the requirements for the specific trains you plan to use before traveling. Now, back to day 1!

The transition is surprisingly dramatic. Tokyo's dense urban landscape gives way to the mountainous Higashi-Ome and Okutama region, where the route winds past deep gorges, rivers, forests and mountain lakes.

It was a fitting introduction to the style of riding we'd encounter throughout the trip: substantial climbing, small mountain roads and long stretches surrounded by forest.

One useful landmark along the route is Lake Okutama, which provides a good opportunity for a bathroom and refueling stop. We reached the high point of our day - Yanagisawa Pass - and enjoyed a warm bowl of soba.

From the mountains, we eventually descended into the lush Kofu Valley and continued toward Yamanashi.

Food: soba at pass, bakery stop, ice cream, hostel dinner and breakfast, konbini stops

Where we stayed: We stayed at Mekiki Hostel, a traditional Japanese home that offered bike parking, lovely meals, and proximity to the nearby Hottarakashi Onsen.

Highlights: Hottarakashi Onsen

One of the highlights of our first day was finishing near Hottarakashi Onsen in Yamanashi. A Japanese onsen is a pretty great reward after more than 7,000 feet of climbing.

Shorter Alternative: If you don't want to begin the trip with nearly 60 miles and 7,000+ feet of climbing, take the train farther to Okutama and begin riding there.

Alternative route: approximately 40.4 miles / 4,798 ft climbing.

From Tokyo, take the Ginza Line to Kanda Station and connect to the Chuo/Ome lines toward Okutama.

This cuts roughly 19 miles and 2,500 feet of climbing from the day while preserving some of the best mountain riding.

Day 2: Yamanashi → Lake Suwa → Kiso-Fukushima

41.4 miles | 2,771 ft climbing | Max grade: 9.8%

Day two begins with a strategic train ride.

We biked approximately two miles to Yamanashi Station and took the Chuo Line to Kami-Suwa Station, a journey of roughly 1 hour and 20 minutes. Trains run relatively frequently, although it's worth confirming schedules before your trip.

From Kami-Suwa, we began riding near Lake Suwa and headed toward the Kiso Valley.

After the climbing on day one, this is a relatively manageable riding day and a chance to enjoy the transition into another distinct part of central Japan.

The destination, Kiso-Fukushima, sits along the historic Nakasendo, the Edo-period highway that connected Edo—modern Tokyo—with Kyoto. The Nakasendo traveled through Japan's central mountains, and remnants of its historic post towns and traditional architecture remain throughout the Kiso Valley.

Highlights: Lake Suwa, Kiso Valley and the historic Nakasendo corridor.

Transit: Yamanashi Station → Kami-Suwa Station on the Chuo Line.

Day 3: Kiso-Fukushima → Takayama

50.8 miles | 7,659 ft climbing | Max grade: 30.8%

This was one of the more serious mountain days of the trip.

Leaving Kiso-Fukushima, we headed deeper into the mountains, following remote “rindo” roads through the Kiso and Hida regions toward Takayama.

Kiso-Fukushima's location on the historic Nakasendo gives the beginning of the ride a tangible connection to Japan's past. But it doesn't take long before the route becomes much more about the mountains themselves.

Expect significant climbing, remote stretches and some extremely steep pitches.

Eventually, the route descends toward Takayama, one of the destinations we were most excited to reach.

Takayama is famous for its beautifully preserved old town and a sake-making tradition stretching back centuries. After a long mountain day, wandering through town—and sampling some local sake—is an excellent way to spend the evening.

Highlights: Historic Nakasendo, remote mountain riding, Takayama's preserved old town, local sake and Sakurayama Hachimangu Shrine.

Worth knowing: Don't let the roughly 50-mile distance fool you. The elevation makes this a substantially harder day than the mileage suggests.

Day 4: Takayama → Shirakawa-go

43.6 miles | 4,749 ft climbing | Max grade: 11%

From Takayama, we made another push into the mountains of Gifu Prefecture.

The standard route is approximately 44 miles with just under 5,000 feet of climbing. There is also a significantly longer option of approximately 61 miles and 7,410 feet of climbing for anyone looking for an even bigger day.

After lunch, the route includes one more substantial climb before beginning the approach toward Shirakawa-go.

And the approach is part of what makes this day special.

Rather than arriving along the main tourist corridor, we descended toward Shirakawa-go on a remote mountain road before reaching the village along the Shogawa River Valley.

A UNESCO World Heritage Site, Shirakawa-go is famous for its gassho-zukuri farmhouses, whose dramatically pitched thatched roofs were designed to withstand the region's heavy winter snow. Some of the houses are more than 250 years old.

Highlights: Remote Gifu mountain roads and Shirakawa-go.

Standard route: 43.6 miles / 4,749 ft

Long option: 61 miles / 7,410 ft

Staying overnight is particularly worthwhile because it gives you the chance to experience the village outside the busiest daytime tourist hours.

Day 5: Shirakawa-go → Kaga

108 miles | 9,121 ft climbing | Max grade: 22.8%

Difficulty: Moderate

On paper, this is the monster day.

At roughly 108 miles with more than 9,000 feet of climbing, the full route from Shirakawa-go to Kaga is easily the biggest day of the trip.

It's also an extraordinary route.

The terrain includes punchy climbs, fast and twisting descents, waterfalls, rice fields, temples and increasingly remote roads. Toward the end of the day, the route narrows onto a tiny mountain path too small for cars.

This is also bear country, so riders should research current wildlife guidance and carry appropriate safety equipment.

Eventually, the route brings you into Kaga, an area known for its hot springs—a particularly appropriate destination after a century ride through the mountains.

The Alternative

Thankfully, there is a very useful Plan B.

Rather than riding the full 108 miles to Kaga, follow the route approximately 68 miles to Ono and take the train the remainder of the way to Kaga.

Full route: 108 miles / 9,121 ft

Shorter option: approximately 68 miles / 6,107 ft before taking the train

I'd strongly recommend keeping this alternative in your plans even if you intend to ride the entire route. Weather, daylight, mechanical problems and fatigue can all turn an ambitious century ride into something considerably less enjoyable.

Highlights: Waterfalls, rice fields, temples, mountain roads, tiny vehicle-free paths and Kaga's hot springs.

Day 6: Kaga → Gamo

42 miles | 2,251 ft climbing | Max grade: 8.3%

After the enormous mountain riding of day five, the route changes character dramatically.

We rolled south from Kaga and reached the Sea of Japan, with one of our first major stops at Yushima Bridge. From there, you can cross to the island and visit its Shinto shrine.

We then spent much of the remainder of the day cycling along the Sea of Japan coast.

At approximately 42 miles with 2,250 feet of climbing, this is one of the easier days of the trip and a welcome opportunity to recover.

It also illustrates what makes the overall route so interesting. In less than a week, we'd gone from Tokyo to mountain lakes, historic valleys, high mountain passes, traditional villages and finally the coast of the Sea of Japan.

Highlights: Yushima Bridge, Shinto shrine and Sea of Japan coastal riding.

Day 7: Gamo → Mihama

45 miles | 1,551 ft climbing | Max grade: 5.1%

Day seven is another relatively gentle stage and, conveniently, the easiest climbing day of the trip.

The route continues south from Gamo toward Mihama, with approximately 45 miles and 1,550 feet of climbing.

After several extremely demanding mountain stages, having two consecutive lower-elevation days before the final push to Kyoto worked well.

Don't mistake "easier" for uninteresting, either. One of the joys of bike touring is that these quieter transitional days often give you more time to notice the places you're riding through rather than focusing entirely on the next climb.

By the time we reached Mihama, we had just one riding day left.

Day 8: Mihama → Kyoto

87 miles | 6,028 ft climbing | Max grade: 15.1%

The final day is not a ceremonial cruise into Kyoto.

At approximately 87 miles with more than 6,000 feet of climbing, it's another substantial ride.

The route heads deep into the mountains north of Kyoto, following tiny roads through dense forests and traditional rural communities. Centuries-old farmhouses dot parts of the landscape and give this portion of the route a completely different atmosphere from Japan's major cities.

Around 43 miles into the ride, a useful food stop is Morishige, a soba restaurant in the Miyama area.

From there, we still had more climbing ahead of us.

Eventually, the route provides views across the Yamashiro Basin, signaling that Kyoto is finally getting close. After one final series of climbs and descents, we entered the city.

After eight days of riding, arriving in Kyoto under our own power felt completely different from simply stepping off the Shinkansen.

We had made it.

Highlights: Rural northern Kyoto, forest roads, traditional farmhouses, mountain scenery, Yamashiro Basin views and finally rolling into Kyoto.

Food stop: Morishige soba, around mile 43.

And if you need another reason to celebrate, Kyoto is a pretty great place to eat after riding almost 90 miles.

How Hard Is This Route?

Short answer: very.

The individual mileage numbers don't tell the whole story. The route combines substantial elevation, steep grades, remote roads and several consecutive days in the mountains.

The three biggest days are particularly notable:

Day 3: 50.8 miles + 7,659 ft

Day 5: 108 miles + 9,121 ft

Day 8: 87 miles + 6,028 ft

Day 5 in particular requires some serious endurance.

That said, one of the advantages of cycling in Japan is the transportation network. Our itinerary deliberately included trains and alternate routes, which provided ways to shorten days without abandoning the overall journey.

I'd recommend approaching this as a bike adventure through Japan rather than an exercise in riding every possible mile.

Using Trains With Your Bike

Public transportation was an intentional part of our route.

We used trains to skip riding through parts of metropolitan Tokyo and to connect Yamanashi with Lake Suwa. We also identified rail options that could shorten particularly difficult days.

Before attempting the trip, research Japan's current rules for transporting bicycles on trains. Depending on your bike and the rail operator, you may need to disassemble or partially disassemble the bike and carry it in a designated bike or rinko bag.

This is something worth practicing before the trip. You don't want your first attempt at packing a bike for a Japanese train to happen on a station platform while you're tired and trying to make a departure.

Navigation & Route Planning

We used both Ride with GPS and Strava to plan and record the trip.

Interestingly, the two platforms didn't always agree on elevation. The copious mountain tunnels scattered throughout the Japanese Alps and the Sea of Japan’s coast also makes it difficult to plot accurate numbers.

For that reason, treat all elevation numbers in this guide as estimates.

More importantly, download your routes for offline use. Many of the best sections of this trip are on small mountain and forest roads where relying entirely on cellular service isn't something I'd recommend.

Building Your Own Version of the Trip

One of my biggest recommendations is not to treat this itinerary as all-or-nothing.

The route works because Japan's rail system gives you options.

If 108 miles and 9,000 feet of climbing sounds great, ride the full Day 5. If it sounds miserable, ride 68 miles and catch the train. If you want a gentler introduction, start Day 1 in Okutama instead of Ome.

The goal isn't to replicate our Strava file perfectly.

The goal is to use the bike to connect places you might otherwise pass straight through.

Eight consecutive days of riding works, but a rest day would give you more time to experience the places you're traveling through and provide some flexibility if weather disrupts an earlier stage.

Why This Route?

Japan is incredibly easy to experience by train. That's precisely why traveling part of it by bicycle felt so special.

The Shinkansen can whisk you from Tokyo to Kyoto in a few hours. We spent eight days getting there.

Along the way, we climbed through the mountains around Okutama, rode beside Lake Suwa, followed the historic Nakasendo into the Kiso Valley, crossed the mountains toward Takayama, descended into Shirakawa-go, rode through rice fields and tiny mountain roads toward Kaga, reached the Sea of Japan, and eventually climbed through the forests north of Kyoto.

By the time we finally rolled into Kyoto, the distance between Tokyo and Kyoto no longer felt abstract. We'd watched the landscape change mile by mile.

And that, more than being able to say we biked from Tokyo to Kyoto, was the point.
